# The Dynamic Agency Dispatch | Issue 086, marketing newsletter

> [!INFO] Cross-Vault Architecture Connections
> **Related System Modules:** [[31_Visual_Diff_Report]]

You’ve been compared to three other agencies this week.
​
All of them are cheaper. All of them “do the same thing.”
​
Except they don’t. Your approach is completely different.
​
But the prospect can’t see it. To them, you’re all in the same bucket.
​
Here’s what’s actually happening:
​
You think you have a positioning problem.
​
You don’t.
​
You have a context problem.
​
Your prospects are comparing you to everyone else because they’re using the only framework they have to evaluate agencies.
​
They don’t understand your “new category” or your “innovative approach.”
​
So their brain does what it always does when it encounters something unfamiliar.
​
It forces you into a box they already understand.
The category creation myth
“We’re creating a new category.”
​
I hear this all the time from agencies trying to differentiate.
​
And it almost never works.
​
Creating a new category sounds great in theory.
​
You’re not competing. You’re inventing a whole new space where you’re the only player.
​
The problem? Your prospects have no idea what you’re talking about.
​
When you say “we’re a growth acceleration consultancy” or “we do full-funnel revenue orchestration,” their brain doesn’t know where to file that.
​
So they do what brains always do.
​
They force you into a box they already understand.
​
“Oh, so you’re a marketing agency.”
​
“Got it, you do lead gen.”
​
You tried to create a new category. They ignored it and categorized you anyway.
Why this happens
People don’t buy things they can’t categorize.
​
Your prospect’s brain is constantly comparing new information to existing frameworks.
​
When they hear your positioning, they’re asking:
​
“What is this similar to?”
​
“Who else does something like this?”
​
“How does this compare to what I already know?”
​
They’re using context to make sense of what you’re saying.
​
And if your positioning doesn’t fit into any existing context, they either ignore it or reshape it until it does.
Context determines positioning
Most agencies do this backwards: They build their positioning first, then try to force it onto the market.
​
But positioning doesn’t exist in a vacuum.
​
It exists in the context your prospects already have.
​
If your market thinks about the problem in terms of “we need more leads,” you can’t just show up and say “actually, you need revenue orchestration.”
​
They don’t know what that means.
​
They’re going to translate it back to “more leads” because that’s the context they have.
​
Your fancy positioning gets ignored.
The two paths forward
Once you understand this, you have two options:
​
Option 1: Match your positioning to their existing context
​
Figure out how your market already thinks about the problem.
​
What language do they use? What categories do they already understand? What comparisons do they naturally make?
​
Then position yourself within that context.
​
You’re not creating a new category. You’re claiming a differentiated position inside an existing one.
​
“We’re the SEO agency that prioritizes revenue over rankings.”
​
They know what SEO is. They know what revenue means. They can categorize this immediately.
​
And the contrast (revenue vs rankings) gives you differentiation without confusion.
​
(I wouldn't try using that one. It's an example, and plenty of SEO agencies are making that claim.)
​
Option 2: Find a market with the right context
​
If you’re committed to your positioning, find prospects who already have the context to understand it.
​
Don’t try to sell “attribution modeling services” to companies still using spreadsheets.
​
Sell it to companies that already know attribution is broken and are actively looking for a solution.
​
Your positioning stays the same. You just changed who you’re talking to.
The bottom line
If prospects keep lumping you in with cheaper competitors, it’s not because they don’t see your value.
​
It’s because they don’t have the context to categorize you differently.
​
Stop trying to create categories your prospects don’t understand.
​
Start working with the context they already have.
​
Context isn’t optional. It’s the foundation.
​
